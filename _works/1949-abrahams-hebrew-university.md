I suppose few of my readers will recognise the initials I.A. or remember the name or the personality behind them, but I have used them deliberately in order to call the attention of our younger generation to an Anglo-Jewish University man who did more than anyone else in his day to keep alive a spirit which can fairly be called humanist, I do not know whether his books are still read. They certainly deserve to be. Even his occasional trifles are of interest; and he had a gift of treating serious matters with a deftness of touch which reminds the reader of the medicine of one novel a day which he used to take regularly after lunch. Israel Abrahams was not a great philospher, or a great philologist, or a great Talmudist, but he was very nearly a great man, and he was that because of all the Jewish scholars of his day he was one of the few who possessed not only scholarship but vision. His attitude to the second part of my title offers an instructive instance.

We have all been brought up to believe that the idea of the Hebrew University of Jerusalem is due to Hermann Schapira of Heidelberg. I once looked up the reference and found to my surprise that Hermann Schapira had no such idea. He envisaged a *German* University for Jews to be set up *somewhere in the settlements!* Even the Weizmann-Feivel-Buber plan of 1901-3 for a Juedische Hochschule only sought to provide a substitute for Jewish students deprived of the chance of higher technical education in Russia and Rumania. It would appear that the idea of a Hebrew University of Jerusalem, that is, a University which should be a University, and which should be in Hebrew and in Jerusalem "as of right" (as it were) and "not on sufferance," a University which should be not only for Jewish students but for Judaism in its original creative language and in its original creative home—this idea was first given publicity (it would seem) by I.A., and in the columns of the "Jewish Chronicle" (28 February, 1908).

I.A. saw that the kernel of the University should be Jewish studies and hoped that in Jerusalem there would be a concentration of available talent, but at the same time he understood that a University must be a University and contain all faculties. He was strongly opposed, as in practice so in theory, to the isolation of Jewish studies, and he saw clearly that the very idea of a University precluded a college confined to Rabbinics or even to humanistic subjects. There was evidently at the time a "narrower " and a "wider" scheme, and I.A. was for the "wider." That is what one would have expected, and we need only note the fact. What is surprising is that, whereas Hermann Schapira (writing in a Hebrew paper) and the authors of the 1901-3 scheme seem to have been convinced that a University must be run in German, and even Schapira himself could only envisage Hebrew becoming a spoken language as a future possibility, I.A., writing, it is true, in 1908, had no doubt at all that the language of the University should be Hebrew. "In Jerusalem of course," he writes "the language of tuition and intercourse would be Hebrew"; and adds: "this surely would be of itself an inestimable gain."

A leading article in the same issue of the "Jewish Chronicle" strongly supported the plan for a Jerusalem University, and I quote a central passage: —

> In a great Jewish University at the very centre of our people's aspirations and hopes, it is quite possible there may be found the solvent of many problems, religious, social, political, economic, which to-day confront us. A new era may begin for Jewry, a new era ushered in by the best traditions which Jewry has ever cherished. For a Jewish University would take its place, and we venture to think a very high place, among the seats of learning of the world. This in its turn could not fail to enhance the status of the Jew wherever he may be, by at once linking him in the world's estimate with something tangibly great and noble and associating him with something big and worthy which would elevate his aspirations and enrich his ideals.

I presume that such writing would be considered now out of date. Few among us now care to be thought to care for "ideals," and few care for what "the world" thinks. But does it not remain true even to-day (or perhaps especially to-day) that, quite apart from the estimate of the world, we should strive, for the sake of our own souls, to be linked with the great and noble?
